The 1/3 Rule for Pressure Cooking

Almost every stovetop-to-pressure-cooker conversion starts from one friendly ratio: pressure cooking takes about one-third of the stovetop time. A beef stew that simmers for 90 minutes on the stove is typically done in 30 minutes at pressure; dried beans that need an hour become a 20-minute affair. The reason is physics, not magic — a sealed cooker holds steam at around 250°F (121°C) instead of boiling point, so heat drives into food dramatically faster.

That ratio is a starting point, not a law. Dense foods like large roasts convert closer to 40 percent of stovetop time, while delicate vegetables can go even faster than one-third before they turn to mush. This calculator uses the 1/3 rule as its backbone and then layers on the practical adjustments — liquid, release method, and ingredient quirks — that separate a decent conversion from a dinner you would proudly serve.

Keep one more number in your back pocket for mental math: the slow cooker sits at the other end of the spectrum. Roughly speaking, 1 hour of stovetop equals 4 hours on slow-cooker high or 8 hours on low. Those ratios let you hop between any two methods without hunting for a new recipe.

Stovetop to Pressure: Worked Examples

Let us run three everyday conversions so the pattern sticks. A chicken soup that simmers 45 minutes on the stove converts to about 15 minutes at pressure, plus a 10-minute natural release for the meat — total hands-off time around 25 minutes versus 45. A pot roast braised 3 hours (180 minutes) becomes roughly 60 minutes at pressure with natural release; the connective tissue still gets the long, moist heat it needs, just at higher temperature.

A risotto stirred for 25 minutes on the stove becomes about 8 minutes at pressure with quick release — and honestly, pressure-cooker risotto is one of the conversions that improves on the original, since the sealed pot loses almost no liquid to evaporation. Notice the pattern in each case: convert the time, then add the release, because food keeps cooking while pressure drops.

Going the other direction is just as useful. A pressure-cooker recipe calling for 12 minutes at pressure translates to about 35–40 minutes on the stove. Multiply by three and round to a sensible number; nobody needs 36 minutes when 35 works.

Slow Cooker Conversions: Low vs High

The slow cooker’s two settings are not as different as people think. High is roughly twice as fast as low — 4 hours on high equals about 8 hours on low for most braises and stews. That means converting from stovetop gives you two answers: multiply stovetop hours by 4 for high, by 8 for low. A 1-hour stovetop chili becomes 4 hours on high or 8 on low.

Pressure to slow cooker is the conversion people get wrong most. Twenty minutes at pressure is roughly 60–75 minutes of stovetop-equivalent cooking, which maps to about 4–5 hours on slow-cooker low. Do not invert the 1/3 rule blindly here — think in stovetop minutes as the common currency, exactly what this calculator does under the hood.

One honest caveat: slow cookers vary wildly. An older, gentle cooker and a modern hot-running one can differ by an hour on the same recipe. The first time you convert a recipe to your slow cooker, check for doneness 30–60 minutes before the converted time and take notes. Your cooker’s personality matters more than any chart.

Liquid: The Non-Negotiable Rule

Here is where conversions actually fail. A pressure cooker must have at least 1 cup (240 ml) of thin liquid — water, broth, or juice — to build steam. Stovetop recipes that rely on a thick sauce or a dry roast will scorch and trigger the burn warning if you convert the time but not the liquid. The fix: add the cup of thin liquid for the pressure phase, then thicken afterward by simmering with the lid off or stirring in a slurry.

Dairy, coconut milk, and thick tomato paste are the usual suspects — they scorch under pressure. Add them after cooking whenever a recipe calls for them early. The same goes for cornstarch and flour thickeners: they belong in the last five minutes, not the sealed phase.

Going the other way, slow cookers need less liquid than stovetop recipes, not more. A sealed lid means almost zero evaporation, so cut the liquid by about a third when converting stovetop to slow cooker — otherwise you get soup when you wanted stew. Vegetables also release water as they cook, which is another reason converted slow-cooker recipes can surprise you.

Natural vs Quick Release, Decoded

The release method is part of the cook time, and choosing wrong ruins texture. Natural release (letting the pot depressurize on its own, 10–15 minutes) keeps cooking food gently — perfect for meats, beans, grains, and anything foamy or starchy that would sputter through the valve. Think of it as a built-in resting phase.

Quick release (opening the valve right away) stops the cooking fast. Use it for vegetables, pasta, seafood, and eggs — anything where an extra five minutes means mush. Never quick-release very full pots of soup or starchy foods; the foam can clog the valve. For those, wait 5–10 minutes, then release the rest.

A useful hybrid: 10-minute natural then quick release. It tames foamy foods while still stopping the cook before vegetables dissolve. When converting a recipe, add the release time to your planning — a "20-minute" pressure recipe with natural release is really a 35-minute recipe.

What Doesn’t Convert Well

Honesty matters: some recipes should not be converted. Delicate fish fillets, quick-cooking greens, and dairy-heavy sauces gain nothing from pressure and lose texture. Anything that cooks in under 10 minutes on the stove — a pan sauce, sautéed shrimp — is already faster than the pressure cooker’s heat-up and release cycle.

Baked goods are another no-go for direct conversion; pressure "baking" steams rather than bakes, giving dense, wet results unless the recipe was designed for it. And crispy things stay crispy only outside the sealed pot — convert the braise, then crisp under the broiler or in the air fryer.

Slow cookers have their own blacklist: pasta, rice, and dairy added at the start turn gummy or curdle over 8 hours. Convert the main braise, then stir those in during the last 30 minutes. This is the single most common slow-cooker conversion mistake.

Adjusting for Altitude and Ingredients

Above about 3,000 feet, everything shifts. Water boils cooler, so stovetop and slow-cooker recipes need more time at altitude — but pressure cookers are sealed, so they barely care. That makes the pressure cooker the altitude cook’s best friend: your converted pressure times stay nearly the same while stovetop times stretch.

Ingredient size matters as much as method. Doubling a roast’s weight does not double pressure time — it adds roughly 5–10 minutes per extra pound for dense meats, because heat penetration, not total mass, is the limit. Cut large pieces into uniform chunks and your converted time stays predictable.

Frozen meat is the exception people ask about: pressure cookers handle it fine (add about 50% more time), while slow cookers should not take frozen meat at all — it lingers in the bacterial danger zone too long. Convert accordingly, and never convert a frozen-meat recipe to the slow cooker.

Common Conversion Mistakes

Mistake one: forgetting the pressurizing time. A full pot can take 15–20 minutes to reach pressure; your "30-minute" dinner is 50 minutes door to plate. Plan for it, especially with frozen ingredients or very full pots.

Mistake two: converting the liquid 1:1. As covered above, pressure needs a minimum of thin liquid and slow cookers need less than the original. Get the liquid right and nine out of ten conversions just work.

Mistake three: browning skips. Converted recipes still need the sear — brown meat on sauté mode before pressure cooking, or the stew tastes flat. The Maillard reaction does not happen at pressure the same way. And mistake four: salting exactly like the original. Less evaporation means less concentration, so pressure and slow-cooker conversions often need a touch more salt at the end. Taste, then season.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert stovetop time to Instant Pot time?

Divide the stovetop simmer time by three. A 60-minute stovetop braise becomes about 20 minutes at high pressure. Then add the release: 10–15 minutes natural release for meats and beans, quick release for vegetables. Make sure the pot has at least 1 cup of thin liquid.

How long is 8 hours on slow cooker low in the Instant Pot?

About 20–25 minutes at high pressure for most stews and braises, plus natural release. Think in stovetop equivalents: 8 hours on low is roughly 1 hour of stovetop simmering, and one-third of that is your pressure time.

Do I need to change the liquid when converting recipes?

Yes. Pressure cookers need at least 1 cup of thin liquid to build steam — add water or broth and thicken after cooking. Slow cookers need about a third less liquid than stovetop recipes because almost nothing evaporates.

What is the difference between natural and quick release?

Natural release lets pressure drop on its own over 10–15 minutes and keeps cooking food gently — best for meats, beans and grains. Quick release vents steam immediately to stop cooking — best for vegetables, pasta and seafood.

Can I convert any recipe to the pressure cooker?

Most braises, soups, stews, beans and grains convert beautifully. Skip delicate fish, quick sautés under 10 minutes, dairy-heavy sauces added at the start, and anything meant to be crispy — those are worse under pressure.

Why did my converted recipe trigger a burn warning?

Almost always too little thin liquid or thick sauces on the bottom. Add at least a cup of water or broth, keep tomato paste and dairy for after pressure cooking, and deglaze the pot after sautéing so nothing is stuck to the base.
